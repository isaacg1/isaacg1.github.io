In [my previous essay](2026/07/15/adhd-and-avoidance), I named the void.
In this piece, I will refine the concept,
and walk through a system that I'm building to identify people who have a void problem,
like me,
and build my philosophy of what recovery will look like.

I'd love to set up a void recovery group, if any of you all are interested. If you are, please get in touch! I could really use to talk with people who are going through the same thing and want to figure out together what change might look like.

## A refined concept of the void

The void is spiral of emotions, beliefs, and actions.
Its progression is as follows:

1. An important task. I care deeply about this task. Doing it and doing it well are tied closely to my self-identity and wider goals. The task is:
    * Long: too long to be completed in a single rush.
    * Individual: it's not a task that is inherently collaborative.
    * Unstructured: it's not a task that is externally structured into smaller components
    * Unfamiliar: I haven't done it so much that I feel like I know what I'm doing.
2. Initial emotional setbacks. First attempts at direct progress don't go so well -- false starts, uncertainty, etc.
3. Avoidance as a coping mechanism. Note, this is different from just falling off the topic from disinterest, ADHD-style. The avoidance actively provides a short-term emotional benefit from covering up the setbacks. Avoidance might consist of:
    * Diverting activities, like phone puzzles, games, etc.
    * Reading articles
    * Watching videos
    * Scrolling
    * Sitting in the bathroom
    * Chatting with friends
    * Going for walks
    * Lying on the floor
    * etc.
    In all cases, avoidance is in proximity to working on the task - interspersed with thinking about returning to the task, internal planning, recriminations, etc.
4. Negative self-emotions towards having avoided. Shame, frustration.
5. Further avoidance, as a further coping mechanism.
6. Pouring more time into the proximity of the task, which ends up as further avoidance.
7. Occasional desperate attempts at direct progress on the task to relieve negative self-emptions. Ends up manifesting very perfectionisticly, because indirect and partial progress doesn't immediately relieve the negative self-emotions.
8. Further avoidance as a coping strategy. The core complement of negative self-emotions being avoided is summarized by the acronym FAHDSED:
* Frustration
* Anger
* Hopelessness
* Despair
* Shame
* Emptiness
* Dissociation
9. Negative self beliefs:
* I am lazy, I don't care enough
* This will never get better
* I'm not cut out for this line of work
* I am fundamentally broken, and need to be reshaped on a fundamental level
* I can't let anyone else see how deeply I am failing
* Only by pouring more time and energy and willpower into this task will I emerge
10. Lashing out at people attempting to provide help, as my self-frustration, self-anger, and shame turn outward. If I have done this to you, I deeply apologize. I am working to change.
11. A mindset of complete dualism: Only direct and immediate progress is worth anything, everything else is laziness and failure. Not even considering indirect progress, reaching out for substantive aid, etc.
12. More avoidance.

This is the void.
It sucks me in, and I spiral deeper and deeper into it.
It is deeply debilitating, and simultaneously very familiar to me.
Note that ADHD is often a component of the spiral for me, as well as an initial trigger,
but it is not essential to the pattern.

This is my experience of the void. Yours may look similar, or it may have differences. Nonetheless, if you identify with this experience and want to recovery, I'd love to be in touch.

## Identifying a Void Problem

So, I experience this pattern. But how bad is it, really? Do I have a Problem?

To answer this question, I've been feeling a kinship with the gambling addiction community. I relate to the feeling of trying to solve problems via the pattern that created them, and of turning to something I find shameful for emotional comfort. There are obviously many important differences, but I do believe there are important overlaps as well.

I therefore decided to adapt [Gamblers Anonymous' 20 questions](https://gamblersanonymous.org/20-questions/) for identifying compulsive gambling.
Here, then, are my 20 questions for identifying a Problem with the Void, as well as my answers:

1. Did you ever lose time from work or school due to the void? <br>
  Yes. Hours spent lying on the floor, doing phone puzzles, in proximity to work without ever touching it.

2. Has the void ever made your home life unhappy? <br>
  Yes. I would sacrifice more and more time and energy to the void, and my interpersonal relationships would suffer immensely.

3. Did the void affect your reputation? <br>
  Yes. The way I blew up at a senior colleague has left him on edge around me in a way I deeply regret, and I am only slowly starting to repair. A previous employer was also nervous about me due to the void, though more about my potentially not being cut out for my preferred line of work.

4. Have you ever felt remorse after voiding? <br>
  Yes. That is my typical experience after voiding.

5. Did you ever void to dig yourself out of a hole of responsibilities? <br>
  Yes. That is my typical reason for voiding.

6. Has the void caused a decrease in your ambition or efficiency? <br>
  Yes. It's made me feel like I'm not cut out to be a professor, which has been my dream job since I was 10. And my efficiency is terrible when I'm the depths of the void.

7. After a period in the void,
did you feel you must return to the void as soon as possible to catch up on your duties? <br>
  Yes. It has simultaneously consumed my life and represented the only way out I could imagine.

8. After completing a duty via the void, did you have a strong urge to enter the void again for another duty? <br>
  Yes. I felt so far behind and so desperate that no completion ever felt like enough. I'd feel sad after finishing and void out more.

9. Did you often void until the deadline had arrived? <br>
  Yes. This recently happened with an attempt at my grant proposal. I only gave up on the attempt because the deadline had arrived.

10. Did you ever ask for favors from others to have more time to feed the void? <br>
  No. I haven't yet pulled others into the void with me.

11. Did you ever sacrifice enjoyable or desirable activities to the void? <br>
  Yes. I didn't maintain connections with friends, and I stopped trying new things.

12. Were you reluctant to stop voiding to do normal actions like eating, using the bathroom, or sleeping? <br>
  Yes. I wouldn't go home to eat dinner because that was giving up. Sure, I was lying on the floor, avoiding and stewing in self-anger, but I couldn't give up. I disrupted sleep in the same way, especially as a PhD student. Also, when I was extremely tired, my brain quieted and direct progress became easier, briefly. The bathroom is an avoidance trigger and technique, so it became part of the void as well.

13. Did the void make you careless of the welfare of yourself or your family? <br>
  No, except in indirect "lack of self-care" ways. But no. I don't think it spread that far.

14. Did you ever void longer than you'd planned? <br>
  Yes. I'd plan to be at work until 6, then stay till 7, 8, 10, many nights. 1 or 2am, on the worst nights.

15. Have you ever voided to escape worry, trouble, boredom, loneliness, or loss? <br>
  No. Some of my void triggers are emotional, but not those emotions.

16. Have you ever committed or considered committing an illegal act as part of the void? <br>
  Perhaps. I'm not sharing details.

17. Did the void cause you to have trouble sleeping? <br>
  Yes. I'd burn so much time and willpower voiding that I would forget to sleep and stay up half the night or fall asleep awkwardly on the couch.

18. Do arguments, disappointments, or frustrations create within you an urge to void? <br>
  Yes. Receiving negative feedback on the work I've done creates an impulse to lash out in anger. My discomfort and shame from feeling and expressing anger leads me back into the void.

19. Did you ever have an urge to celebrate good fortune by voiding? <br>
  No. After a helpful conversation, I might work in a good headspace for a few hours when I wouldn't usually be working, and then void afterwards, but that feels different.

20. Have you ever considered self destruction or suicide as a result of the void? <br>
  No.

To identify a Void Problem, count the number of "Yes" answers. If there's at least 9, that's probably a Void Problem.

The GA threshold is 7, but I'm using 9 as a threshold for identifying a Void Problem, because there's a couple of questions about nonstandard relationships with gambling that translate into more standard relationships with the void.

I have 14 "Yes", 1 "Perhaps", and 5 "No". I have a Void Problem.

If you have a Void Problem, please reach out to me. Let's try to figure this out together.

## What are we going to do?

I don't know what Void recovery looks like. I haven't recovered, and I don't know anyone who has. But I do have ideas:

1. Self-forgiveness. Shame, frustration, and self-anger are key components of the void spiral. I can sap their power.
2. Recognition. If I can recognize the void beginning to spiral, I can head it off.
3. Identification of triggers. If I know when I begin to enter the void, I can address it at that point.
4. Co-working. I have a system: Discuss a plan for a working increment with co-worker(s), then attempt that plan for 25 minutes according to a timer. Then check in about how it went and the plan for the next increment, and go again. "Pomodoro" or "Tomato" are names for some parts of this system.
5. Connecting with others who experience the void. It helps to not feel so alone.
6. Analyzing the void. I'm a very analytical person, and analyzing the void makes it feel a bit more under control.

These are my ideas. I'd love to hear yours. Please get in touch. Let's recover together.
